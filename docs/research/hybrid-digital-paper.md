# Hybrid digital, paper and screenless learning

Reviewed: 2026-09-29. **The app optimizes learning, not screen time.** This is a product objective, not an empirical effect estimate.

## Media support processes; paper is not generally better — MODERATE

**Evidence.** Reading-medium studies concern comprehension of text. Delgado et al.'s meta-analysis reports a paper advantage with moderators including time pressure and text genre. This cannot establish a paper advantage for Mandarin pronunciation, character motor learning or interactive feedback. The [Chinese handwriting/typing review](hanzi-recognition-writing.md) addresses different mappings, not merely screen versus paper.

**Product interpretation.** Digital strengths include audio, scheduling, feedback, scaffolding and exploration. Paper/off-screen opportunities include physical reconstruction, handwriting and recall without visible answers. A stylus on a screen also involves motor production; paper with a model can still be heavily scaffolded.

**Limits.** Separate medium, task, feedback, input tool and assistance. Our digital → paper combination has not been shown superior in a controlled adult-Mandarin trial.

**Source.** Delgado, Vargas, Ackerman & Salmerón (2018), *Don't throw away your printed books: A meta-analysis on the effects of reading media on reading comprehension*, Educational Research Review 25, 23–38, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2018.09.003). Publisher text unavailable in this review; bounded finding checked against the [research team's university report](https://www.uv.es/uvweb/uv-noticias/ca/noticies/eri-lectura-universitat-demostra-llegir-paper-es-mes-efectiu-digital-1285973304159/Novetat.html?id=1286054372857&plantilla=UV_Noticies%2FPage%2FTPGDetaillNews). Do not derive quantitative product promises from this access level.

## Offloading and distraction are different mechanisms — MODERATE

**Evidence.** Risko & Gilbert review how external actions/aids redistribute cognitive work. Offloading can be useful; it is not equivalent to laziness or memory damage. Sana et al.'s classroom experiment found a comprehension cost from laptop multitasking, including for nearby peers. It tests divided attention in lectures, not quiet single-task PWA use.

**Product interpretation.** Let the app remember schedules while learners reconstruct the target language. Withhold the answer during recall, retain accessible help, and keep avoidable interruptions low. Screenless recall is a plausible opportunity for independent reconstruction.

**Limits.** Neither source proves “screens reduce learning,” that all switching is harmful, or that our uncluttered UI improves retention. No need to add tracking of distractions. Screenless self-report remains unverified evidence.

**Sources.** Risko & Gilbert (2016), *Cognitive Offloading*, Trends in Cognitive Sciences 20, 676–688, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2016.07.002), [abstract checked](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27542527/?dopt=Abstract). Sana, Weston & Cepeda (2013), *Laptop multitasking hinders classroom learning for both users and nearby peers*, Computers & Education 62, 24–31, [author institution record/abstract](https://pure.athabascau.ca/en/publications/laptop-multitasking-hinders-classroom-learning-for-both-users-and/).

## Rest and consolidation — MODERATE for the studied conditions

**Evidence.** Humiston et al.'s preregistered replication did not find the predicted benefit of eyes-closed rest. Their accompanying meta-analysis of ten similar studies nevertheless found a positive aggregate verbal-memory effect. This combination supports uncertainty and bounded potential, not a guaranteed rest intervention.

**Product interpretation — LOW / HYPOTHESIS.** Offer a voluntary stopping point while allowing continuation. Do not pretend to detect neural consolidation from task completion.

**Limits.** Quiet waking rest differs from paper writing, an app closure screen or ordinary activity after a session. This evidence supplies no optimal session duration, daily lockout or mandatory break schedule.

**Source.** Humiston, Tucker, Summer & Wamsley (2019), *Resting States and Memory Consolidation: A Preregistered Replication and Meta-Analysis*, Scientific Reports 9, 19345, [publisher full text checked](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-019-56033-6.pdf).

No strong comparative evidence for one universally optimal grid, grid size or sequence was established in this review. This is an evidence gap, not proof that none exists. Guidance can orient production; too much simultaneous visual information may compete for attention, but [Hanzi visual-load evidence](hanzi-recognition-writing.md) does not directly compare these grids.

**Proposed sequence, not implemented:** digital introduction/stroke guidance → paper production with stronger grid support → reduced support → later paper recall with the target hidden → self-comparison and later scheduled retrieval. **Proposed policy:** Paper writing may become **curricularly expected for active Writing Targets**, while remaining **situationally deferrable** (“Später / kein Papier”). This is a future proposal, not a change to current optional Build E behavior. Deferral must not become failure or block unrelated spoken progress.

[Handwriting evidence](hanzi-recognition-writing.md) motivates active production, while [retrieval/spacing evidence](learning-science.md) cautions against treating many immediate copies as delayed recall. No cited evidence establishes massed copying as optimal; no fixed copy quota is justified. Self-comparison is useful practice but not automatic objective handwriting grading.
